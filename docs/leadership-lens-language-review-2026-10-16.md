# Leadership Lens language review — October 16, 2026

Reviewed the published configuration from /api/content?type=leadership-lens and the live introduction. This is a proposed editorial revision, not a validated instrument or a production change.

## Main judgment

The assessment is approachable and useful as a coaching conversation starter. Its biggest opportunity is to distinguish what a leader is protecting internally from the behavior they exhibit. All three centers can analyze, care about people, resist, avoid conflict, or act decisively. Wording should make the underlying concern recognizable without asking participants to know the theory.

Do not promise to identify an Enneagram triad from this short reflection. Integrative9 distinguishes centers of expression, intelligence, and structure, and cautions that visible expression can differ from type-related structure. A stronger claim would require a separate assessment-development and validation process. Keep the public promise at “a leadership perspective to explore.”

Sources: https://www.integrative9.com/enneagram/centers/ and https://www.narrativeenneagram.org/three-centers-of-intelligence/ . The editorial judgments below are recommendations, not findings validated by those sources.

## Priority findings in the published wording

1. Gut high side currently says “When others hesitate, you make the call,” “turn conviction into momentum,” and “things will be handled.” These return to action/decisiveness and conflict with the original brief. Quiet, accommodating, or internally resistant leaders may not recognize themselves.
2. Q1 Heart begins “Is something wrong?” That could attract uncertainty/security concerns scored as Head elsewhere. “[person]” is also an unfinished placeholder.
3. Q1 Head asks “how long I should wait,” which can mean boundary-setting, and “what I need to know,” which is circular. Specify uncertainty and anticipating what comes next.
4. Q1 Gut states that disrespect has occurred. This may capture a reasonable judgment about the boss's behavior rather than a recurring internal pattern. Describe the leader's felt concern instead.
5. Q2 largely repeats Q1. That can be useful triangulation, but it is not independent evidence. Make Q1 attention, Q2 the internal concern that needs settling, and Q3 overreliance explicit.
6. Q3 asks what participants “need to watch,” inviting an assessment of the least desirable behavior. Ask what they actually recognize happening.
7. Q4 uses “criticism” although the scenario only says a colleague questions an approach. This intensifies the situation. Use “question” or “challenge.” Its Gut answer combines fairness, responsibility, and boundaries; simplify to the common concern.
8. Q5 Gut offers an admirable capability, while Head and Heart answers describe reassurance. Make all three choices describe what settles the leader internally.
9. Q6 Heart makes softening one's position central; a leader focused on competence or reputation might instead defend or intensify their position. Include concern about how one is seen without prescribing one behavior.
10. The Head questions emphasize uncertainty and repeated analysis more than searching for possibilities or alternatives. Preserve some space for that expression without making each option a long list.
11. High sides overstate what six answers establish. “Teams ... stay longer,” “before anyone speaks,” and “makes fewer avoidable mistakes” are not demonstrated by this tool. Use “can help” and “may support.”
12. A final reflection can clarify a close result; it cannot establish the participant's “real intention.” If it changes the displayed primary profile, say so plainly and invite the participant to compare both profiles.
13. Saved scoring notes still describe exact ties only. Update those notes to reflect the one-point-lead clarification rule. The public default note is already migrated at render time.
14. Five lenses in the hero are attractive, but may imply five results. Optional: caption the image “Different perspectives reveal different things,” or choose a three-lens visual later if users report confusion.

## Recommended introduction and instructions

Introduction:
“Discover where your attention tends to go first, how that perspective supports your leadership, and what it may cause you to overlook. Inspired by the Enneagram's Head, Heart, and Gut centers, this reflection offers a leadership lens to explore—not an Enneagram type or a fixed identity.”

Instructions:
“Think about what usually happens inside you, before you choose how to respond. Select the answer closest to your experience, even if more than one fits. Focus on the concern underneath your reaction, rather than the response you think a leader should give. There are no better or worse answers.”

For later-pattern questions, add:
“Choose the pattern you recognize most often across similar situations.”

Use “first reaction” for the first-attention questions; use “usual pattern” for the overreliance questions. One instruction should not imply that a reflective question is asking for an instantaneous reaction.

## Proposed question draft

Preserve IDs and mappings if adopted. Labels below are editor-only. These are wording hypotheses to pilot, not claims of improved measured accuracy.

### Scenario 1 — Your boss is late

“You are ready for a scheduled meeting with your boss. They are late, and you have received no update. Think about your usual reaction in similar situations.”

Q1. Before you decide what to do, what gets your attention first?
- Head: “I don't know what has happened or what to expect next.”
- Heart: “I wonder what this says about how much my boss values my contribution.”
- Gut: “I feel that my time and commitments are being taken out of my hands.”

Q2. What would most help you feel settled internally?
- Head: “Enough clarity to understand what is happening and anticipate what comes next.”
- Heart: “Reassurance that my contribution matters and that I am valued.”
- Gut: “A sense that I can choose my own limit and honor my other commitments.”

Q3. When this happens repeatedly, which inner pattern do you recognize most?
- Head: “I keep searching for explanations or possible outcomes because the uncertainty is hard to leave alone.”
- Heart: “I keep returning to what this means about my value or how my boss sees me.”
- Gut: “I hold onto a sense that this is not okay, even when my resistance stays quiet.”

Review Q1 Gut with clients: “taken out of my hands” may feel stronger than lateness warrants. Alternative: “I notice that my time and commitments are being decided around me.” Neither wording should assume the boss intended disrespect.

### Scenario 2 — Your approach is questioned

“An initiative you lead has missed a milestone. During a team meeting, a colleague questions your approach. You will need to continue working together.”

Q4. As your colleague speaks, what gets your attention first?
- Head: “What I may have missed and what information or other explanation could change my understanding.”
- Heart: “How the team is seeing me and what this means for their respect or confidence in me.”
- Gut: “Whether the challenge feels fair or asks me to accept something that does not sit right.”

Q5. What would most help you feel settled afterward?
- Head: “A clearer understanding of what happened and the options available.”
- Heart: “Knowing that I am still valued and respected, even where people disagree with me.”
- Gut: “Knowing that I can stay true to my judgment without being pushed into agreement.”

Q6. If the discomfort continues, which inner pattern do you recognize most?
- Head: “I keep revisiting explanations or alternatives, trying to think my way out of the discomfort.”
- Heart: “I become preoccupied with how others see me and lose touch with my own perspective.”
- Gut: “I become more fixed in what feels right to me, whether I push back openly or resist quietly.”

Potential pilot issue: Q4 Gut may attract any leader who judges a challenge unfair. Ask participants to explain why the concern matters to them; do not assume the selected wording proves the motive.

## Proposed final motivation reflection

Question:
“Across situations like these, which concern is hardest for you to let go of—even when you know you can handle the situation?”

- Head: “Not having enough clarity or options to feel prepared for what might happen.”
- Heart: “Feeling that my value or standing with people who matter to me is in question.”
- Gut: “Feeling pushed to accept something that conflicts with my own sense of what is right.”

Keep only eligible options under the existing close-score rule. For a three-way tie, show all three. Consider whether the close-score reflection should nominate a profile to explore rather than present a definitive primary lens. An optional unscored “both fit” or “neither fits well” path would acknowledge uncertainty rather than manufacture precision; that would require a separate behavior/scoring change.

## Proposed balanced high sides

Head:
“Your curiosity can help a team question assumptions, anticipate risks, and see alternatives. You bring value when you turn information and possibilities into a clearer shared understanding.”

Heart:
“Your awareness of people and meaning can help you notice expectations, recognize contributions, and build connection. You bring value when you stay attentive to others while expressing your own authentic perspective.”

Gut:
“Your internal compass can help you recognize what feels out of alignment and what deserves care or protection. You bring steadiness through attention to principles, boundaries, and commitments. This may show up in a clear stand, quiet consistency, or a thoughtful refusal to compromise what matters.”

Keep the existing low sides and opportunities as a strong starting point. Replace universal “Your team may...” claims with “When this happens, others may...” to tie consequences to overreliance, not identity.

Results framing:
“Your answers suggest a perspective worth exploring.”

Close results:
“Your responses draw on more than one perspective. Your final reflection suggests starting with this profile; compare it with your other leading lens and notice which motivation feels more familiar across situations.”

Consider “consistent response pattern” instead of “Strong preference,” or ensure the existing explanation is prominent: the label concerns agreement across these answers, not confidence that a triad has been identified.

## Opportunities beyond wording

- Both scenarios involve another person appearing to disregard or challenge the participant. A scenario about an opportunity, a strategic choice, or competing commitments would sample leadership beyond interpersonal threat.
- Three questions about one event can reinforce the same answer interpretation. Treat the result as a small contextual sample.
- Ask 8–12 pilot participants to explain each selected answer in their own words before showing results. Include quiet/reserved leaders, leaders who value achievement and reputation, and leaders who manage uncertainty by generating possibilities.
- Listen for the same wording being selected for different reasons. Record confusion and revise one source of ambiguity at a time.
- For participants with an established Enneagram understanding, compare their explanations as exploratory feedback, not a validation study or an assumption that self-reported type is ground truth.
- If formal triad identification becomes the objective, define that change explicitly and develop a broader instrument rather than extending the claim of this six-question tool.
